Carbon Impulse Model
02-120 class project · carbon dioxide model

How much of our CO₂ stays in the air?

When we burn fuel or cut down forests, CO₂ goes into the air. The ocean and plants slowly soak some of it back up, but not all of it. This page uses a published scientific model (Joos et al., 2013) to estimate how much stays in the air, and checks the answer against real measurements.

1. Release carbon. Each year adds a batch of CO₂ to the air.
2. Nature removes some. Part disappears in a few years, part over decades, part over centuries. About a fifth never leaves on human timescales.
3. Add it all up. What's left from every year's batch is the extra CO₂ in the air today.

Units: GtC = billion tonnes of carbon. ppm = parts per million, the unit for CO₂ in the air. 2.124 GtC raises CO₂ by 1 ppm.

What we learned

  • Test 1 passes. The model leaves 23.5% of the carbon after 1,000 years. The paper says about 25%.
  • Test 2 overshoots. With all real emissions, the model predicts 478 ppm for 2024. The real value was 425 ppm.
  • "Fuel only" looks close, but that's luck: it leaves out a third of the real emissions.

Why the model overshoots

  • It was built for today's air, which already has extra CO₂. Today's ocean is fuller, so it soaks up CO₂ more slowly than the ocean of the 1800s did.
  • It ignores that plants grow faster with more CO₂ and so take up more of it.
  • So it removes too little carbon, and more CO₂ stays in the air than really did.

For the math

IRF(t) = 0.2173 + 0.2240·e−t/394.4 + 0.2824·e−t/36.54 + 0.2763·e−t/4.304

IRF(t) is the share of the carbon still in the air t years after it's released. CO₂(year) = starting CO₂ + Σ emissions × IRF(years since emitted) ÷ 2.124. A run starting in 1850 begins at 285 ppm. A later start begins at that year's Mauna Loa value and ignores earlier emissions.

Data: Global Carbon Budget emissions via Our World in Data · Mauna Loa CO₂ (NOAA) · Joos et al. (2013)
