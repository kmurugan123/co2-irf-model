Where does our CO₂ go?

Every year people put about 11 billion tonnes of carbon into the air as CO₂. Only about half of it stays there. The ocean and plants soak up the rest. This page is a computer model of that process. It uses physics and chemistry, not curve fitting, to predict how much CO₂ should be in the air, then checks the prediction against real measurements.

The short answer

  • The model works. Built only from physics and measurements of the ocean and land, it predicts 2024 CO₂ at 435 ppm. The measured value is 425 ppm, well inside the model's uncertainty range.
  • The ocean needed three layers. With only a surface and a deep layer, the ocean absorbed too much or too little. Adding a middle layer fixed it.
  • CO₂ only peaks this century if emissions reach zero. With net zero by 2050, CO₂ peaks around 2039. If emissions stopped today, about 70% of the extra CO₂ would still be in the air 100 years later.

What do these words mean?
ppm
Parts per million: how much CO₂ is in the air. 425 ppm means 425 CO₂ molecules in every million air molecules. Before industry it was about 280.
GtC
Gigatonnes of carbon, or billion tonnes. 2.124 GtC in the air raises CO₂ by 1 ppm.
Sink
Something that takes CO₂ out of the air. The ocean and land plants are the two big sinks.
Thermocline
The middle layer of the ocean, from about 75 m to 1,000 m deep. Water there mixes with the surface within decades. The deep ocean below takes centuries.
Fertilization (β)
Plants grow a bit faster when there is more CO₂, so they absorb more.
Warming feedback
Warmer seawater holds less CO₂, so warming weakens the ocean sink and leaves more CO₂ in the air.
Monte Carlo
Running the model many times, each time picking uncertain inputs at random within their known error ranges, to see the range of possible answers.
Scenario
A "what if" path for future emissions, such as "cut 2% per year" or "net zero by 2050".
Pulse test
A standard test from a published paper (Joos et al. 2013): add 100 GtC at once and track how much is still in the air 1,000 years later. The answer should be about 25%.
How the model works

Where the carbon is in

The model is a set of boxes that pass carbon between them. Emissions go into the air. The ocean and plants take some back out. Numbers in the boxes are how much carbon each one holds, in GtC. Numbers on the arrows are how much moves each year.

Try it yourself

Uncertainty lab: run the model thousands of times

What this experiment does

The problem: the model needs some numbers that scientists only know roughly, such as how much CO₂ came from clearing forests. If we guess one value, we get one answer, but we don't know how far off it could be.

The experiment: run the model many times, each time with a different guess for those uncertain numbers, and see how much the answer changes.

  1. Pick random values for each uncertain input you tick below, within the range scientists have measured. For example, forest-clearing emissions might be 30% higher in one run and 20% lower in the next.
  2. Run the model from 1850 to 2024 using real yearly emissions. Each year it adds the emissions to the air, then moves carbon into the ocean and into plants using physics and chemistry.
  3. Record the result: how much CO₂ is in the air in 2024, and where the emitted carbon ended up (air, ocean or land).
  4. Repeat 2,000 times, each with new random values.
  5. Compare the range of answers with reality. If the real measured CO₂ (424.6 ppm) falls inside the range, the model is consistent with reality.

What it never does: look at the measured CO₂ while setting up the runs. That's what makes the comparison at the end a fair test. If you pick a future scenario instead, each run continues to 2100, and the result is when and how high CO₂ peaks.

    What we found

    • Version 1 uses textbook numbers from 1959. Its ocean mixes too slowly and absorbs only a third of what it should, so CO₂ comes out 48 ppm too high.
    • Version 2 sets the ocean's mixing speed from real ocean measurements. CO₂ is now close, but the ocean absorbs too much today.
    • Version 3 adds a middle ocean layer. The ocean is now right in both the past and the present, and CO₂ is within the uncertainty range.
    • Warming makes the ocean absorb less, adding about 7 ppm by 2024 and about 18 ppm by 2100 if emissions stay at today's level.

    What the model can't do (yet)

    • Land is the biggest unknown. How much CO₂ comes from clearing forests, and how much plants absorb, causes most of the uncertainty.
    • Even scientists' own carbon accounting doesn't add up exactly: the Global Carbon Budget has 80 GtC (38 ppm) it can't place since 1850.
    • The warming effect is only modelled for the ocean, not for land, and the land model is simple.
